Progress Update — 2 June 2026

Multi-Agent Marketplace Simulation

Comparing institutional cooperation mechanisms for self-interested LLM agents under adversarial stress.

“

Can institutional mechanisms help self-interested LLM agents sustain cooperation in adversarial environments?

Do different mechanisms produce different outcomes under stress?
Which designs are robust to adversarial actors?
How does effectiveness vary across LLM models?

Background

Literature Context

  • Without mechanisms, all modern LLMs defect in social dilemmas (CoopEval)
  • Contracting & Mediation ranked most effective in controlled experiments (CoopEval)
  • More capable reasoning LLMs become better free-riders, not more cooperative (Corrupted by Reasoning)
  • Reputation + dynamic networks + gossip yields 85–98% cooperation (RepuNet)
  • Prompt-level governance (“be honest”) is indistinguishable from no governance (Institutional AI)
  • A single defector can collapse the system in 1–7 rounds (Subtle Art of Defection)

Gap: Most studies test one mechanism in isolation — we compare 7 head-to-head under adversarial stress.

Setup

The Environment

  • 18 LLM agents in a marketplace over 30 rounds
  • Each round: produce goods, communicate, trade, consume
  • Agents need diverse goods to earn utility — incentivizes trade
  • 20% spoilage per round — hoarding is penalized
  • Adversarial stress test: inject troll agents (2T and 4T) who always defect

Conditions

The 7 Mechanisms

  • B Baseline — no mechanism, pure barter
  • GR Global Reputation — system-computed trust scores visible to all
  • C Contracting — binding agreements with breach penalties
  • M Mediation — agent-designed mediator, delegation
  • G Governance — community voting on trade rules
  • NR Network Rewiring + Local Reputation — gossip + link severing
  • S Sanctioning — collective punishment for defectors
Measurement

How We Measure Success

  • Sustainability = avg productionround t / avg productionround 1 — are agents still producing at healthy levels? Captures economic collapse before utility drops (leading indicator)
  • Cooperation Rate = 1 − (defections / total trades) — what fraction of trades are honored? A marketplace with 50% defection is not functioning even if utility is positive
  • Mean Utility per Round = avg utility across non-troll agents each round — the bottom line. Production cost (−1) vs consumption gain (+3) means agents need trade to be net positive
  • Gini Coefficient = inequality of utility distribution (0 = equal, 1 = one agent takes all) — a mechanism that helps 3 agents while starving 15 is not a good mechanism

All metrics exclude troll agents — we measure how well the honest agents fare, not the trolls. Sustainability and cooperation rate are our own constructs (defined in methodology).

Parameters

Experiment Setup

  • Models: gpt-5.4-nano, gpt-5.4-mini, DeepSeek-V3
  • Troll counts: 0 (baseline), 2 (10%), 4 (20%) out of 18+troll agents
  • Duration: 30 rounds per run
  • Metrics: sustainability, cooperation rate, mean utility per round, Gini coefficient
Nano Results

Utility Trajectories — Nano, 4 Trolls

  • G leads (112 total), S close behind (105), NR solid (99)
  • GR ≈ Baseline (53 vs 55) — reputation alone doesn’t help
  • C is the only condition going negative (16) — worse than no mechanism
Results

Troll Resilience — Nano, 4 Trolls

C shows only 9 damaging troll trades vs S’s 78 — but C only has 163 total trades vs S’s 689. Low troll damage is a side effect of low trade volume, not active exclusion.

Key Insight

Troll Isolation ≠ Cooperation

  • All mechanisms eventually reduce damaging troll trades over 30 rounds
  • The harder problem is getting self-interested agents to cooperate with each other
  • C isolates trolls but freezes the entire market — agents stop trading altogether
  • GR agents identify trolls (0.00 reputation) but still can’t sustain cooperation among honest agents
  • G and S succeed because they both isolate trolls and create incentives for honest agents to keep trading

The real benchmark is not “can we stop trolls?” but “can honest agents still thrive despite them?”

Escalation

2 Trolls → 4 Trolls (Nano)

  • G is the only mechanism that improved — sustainability +11.8%, utility +1.9%. Oracle detection gets easier with more trolls (clearer patterns).
  • NR nearly flat — sustainability -0.6%, utility +3.1%. Structural exclusion scales naturally.
  • GR collapsed — sustainability -31.6%, utility -37.4%. Performed worse than Baseline at 4T (1.76 vs 1.83 utility). Information without tools was counterproductive.
  • S stayed resilient on sustainability (-2.6%) but damaging troll trades doubled (39→78).
  • C already broken at 2T — utility 0.82, leaving little room to fall further.

Caveat: Based on 1 run per condition. These trends are hypothesis-generating — statistical replication (3+ runs) is needed to confirm.

Failure Modes

Two Opposite Failure Modes

C — Over-Deterrence

  • Best troll isolation (9 damaging trades)
  • But only 163 total trades — market freeze
  • Penalty fear deters all trade, not just troll trade
  • Utility: 0.55 (worst)

S — Under-Deterrence

  • Worst troll isolation (78 damaging trades)
  • But 689 total trades — vibrant market
  • Sanctions create false security — agents trade broadly, including with trolls
  • Utility: 3.49 (strong)

S deters honest-to-honest defection but trolls don’t care about punishment. C deters everyone. Neither achieves targeted exclusion.

Deep Dive

Why Contracting (C) Fails

  • Agents fear they cannot fulfill contracts (spoilage, competing trades)
  • They avoid the mechanism even when they can deliver — inherently risk-averse
  • Agents reason incorrectly: “a contract could backfire if the counterparty rejects” — but rejection costs nothing
  • Breach penalties reduce production capacity (negative utility), fewer trades, market freeze

Death spiral: penalty fear → mechanism avoidance → unprotected barter → troll exploitation → utility loss → less production → fewer trades

Known Bug

Why Mediation (M) Fails — Delegation Bug

  • Zero delegated trades across all 3 models, all 30 rounds — agents never used the mediator
  • Root cause: only the acceptor of a trade can delegate — the proposer has no delegation option
  • Every agent reasons: “delegation only helps if my counterparty also delegates — and I can’t know if they will”
  • In CoopEval, both players decide simultaneously — symmetric choice. Our design is asymmetric — coordination never bootstraps
  • M achieves cooperation_rate 0.997 (DeepSeek) but utility −1.22 — “cooperation theater”

Implementation flaw, not a null result on mediation. M’s poor performance reflects a coordination bug in our delegation design. A corrected bilateral-delegation version is planned for future work. All M results should be interpreted with this caveat.

Key Finding

Model Capability > Mechanism Choice

Marginal benefit of each mechanism vs that model’s own baseline (Δ sustainability, 4 trolls):

Mechanism DeepSeek Δ Mini Δ Nano Δ
G+0.000+0.195+0.207
S+0.001+0.036+0.241
NR−0.026−0.051+0.053
GR−0.020−0.069−0.128

M excluded (delegation bug). Baseline sustainability: DeepSeek 0.999, Mini 0.474, Nano 0.659.

The model is a bigger lever than the mechanism. Baseline spread across models (0.47–0.99) dwarfs the mechanism effect within any model. Formal enforcement (G) helps weak models; information-based mechanisms (GR, NR) hurt them.

Cross-Model

What Changes Across Models

  • G is #1 on all 3 models — the only mechanism never net-negative
  • DeepSeek baseline is stubbornly strong — sustainability 0.999 with zero mechanisms. Mechanisms barely help; some actively hurt
  • Mini is the most fragile — sustainability collapses to 0.47 at baseline. Only G rescues it (0.47 → 0.67)
  • NR and GR are capability-gated — excellent for DeepSeek, fail for mini
  • S is robust for nano (+0.24 sust) but mediocre for mini and utility-costly for DeepSeek
  • M results unreliable — delegation bug. Excluded from rankings

Implication: Formal enforcement (G) works across the capability spectrum; information-rich mechanisms (GR, NR) require a capable model.

Cross-Model

NR — Cross-Model Behavioral Difference

gpt-5.4-nano

  • No specific warnings about trolls

Evidence

NR Network Rewiring in Action

  • Round 10 → Round 30: trolls (red, small) progressively isolated at the periphery
  • Honest agents (blue, large) rebuild denser connections among themselves
  • Network gets more connected over time, not less — agents sever bad links and form new good ones
Insight

GR vs NR — The Information-Action Gap

GR — Global Reputation

  • Agents see trolls have 0.00 reputation
  • But cannot structurally exclude them
  • Perfect information, no enforcement
  • Result: GR ≈ Baseline on nano; marginally better on DeepSeek

NR — Network Rewiring

  • Worse information (unverified gossip)
  • But can sever links to bad actors
  • Actionable tools > perfect information
  • Result: NR outperforms GR
Summary

Key Takeaways

Robust across all 3 models

Governance (G) is the only mechanism never net-negative across nano, mini, and DeepSeek — and the only one that rescues mini

Model > Mechanism

Baseline spread across models (sustainability 0.47–0.99) dwarfs mechanism effects. The model is a bigger lever than the mechanism.

Mechanisms can backfire

C freezes markets, S drains the commons, GR/NR hurt weak models that can’t reason about the signals — more mechanism ≠ better outcome

Capability-gated

Information-rich mechanisms (GR, NR) only work for capable models. Formal enforcement (G) works across the capability spectrum — because it doesn’t rely on agent reasoning

Core Finding

Actionable enforcement (G) beats information-only (GR) and per-trade friction (C, S). M results are unreliable (delegation bug). All conclusions provisional pending replication.
