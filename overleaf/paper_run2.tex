\begin{abstract}
Self-interested agents, left unconstrained, tend toward defection in repeated social dilemmas, causing cooperative surplus to collapse. This paper investigates what formal mechanisms — layered on top of unrestricted communication — are sufficient for a society of such agents to maintain market stability. We instantiate the research question as a multi-agent marketplace simulation where 18 LLM agents with complementary production specialties must trade within a constrained social network to obtain utility. Agents operate in a barter economy where production, trade, and survival depend on managing perishable goods. Cooperation yields a positive-sum surplus; without mechanisms, rational anticipation of defection suppresses trade and drives the society toward autarky. We test three formal mechanisms — Reputation, Contracting, and Mediation — individually and in combination across a $2^3$ factorial design of eight experimental conditions, measuring market stability through two social metrics: Sustainability and Peace. Our results reveal a striking negative finding: no condition achieves market stability, and all mechanisms increase total defections relative to the communication-only baseline. Peace is universally the binding constraint; Reputation improves Sustainability but worsens Peace; Contracting and Mediation are completely unused by agents despite being available. We trace the failure to a homogeneous reasoning framework in which every agent independently concludes defection is the dominant strategy in round one, producing a universal defection equilibrium that formal mechanisms cannot overcome. The Sustainability-Peace tradeoff that emerges --- mechanisms sustaining production while defection proliferates --- represents a novel form of institutional failure in LLM agent societies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================

The stability of cooperative societies is among the oldest problems in social science and, increasingly, in artificial intelligence. As multi-agent systems grow more capable and more autonomous, the question is no longer merely theoretical: what formal structures allow a population of self-interested agents to maintain collective welfare over time?

Prior work in game theory identifies the core tension through \textit{sequential social dilemmas} — situations in which individually rational choices produce collectively suboptimal outcomes \citep{coopeval}. In the canonical Prisoner's Dilemma, two agents each defect because defection dominates cooperation regardless of the other's action, yet mutual defection leaves both worse off than mutual cooperation. Scaling this logic to multi-agent systems produces an analogous result: without intervention, populations converge toward all-defection equilibria.

Recent empirical work confirms that modern large language models (LLMs), despite their social reasoning capabilities, exhibit this same failure mode. \citet{coopeval} show that all tested LLMs defect in every social dilemma without formal mechanisms in place. Communication alone is insufficient. Natural language promises are cheap and unverifiable; threats are credible only through repetition; warnings spread only through voluntary disclosure. The question is therefore not whether communication helps, but \textit{how much formal structure is needed on top of communication} to sustain societal cooperation.

\subsection{Research Question}

\begin{quote}
\textit{What formal mechanisms, layered on top of communication, allow a society of self-interested agents to maintain market stability?}
\end{quote}

We instantiate this question as a \textbf{marketplace society}: a multi-agent environment in which agents hold complementary production specialties and must trade to obtain utility. The marketplace is a natural laboratory for this question because:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Cooperative surplus is structural — every agent benefits from trading, so there is always something to lose from defection.
  \item Defection opportunities arise at every step — production, negotiation, and execution all admit exploitation.
  \item Communication is realistic — agents negotiate, broadcast price signals, and propagate reputation information, mirroring real market behavior.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A concrete simulation environment implementing a marketplace social dilemma with comparative advantage, a barter economy, a constrained social network, and a natural defection surface.
  \item A layered mechanism testing framework that benchmarks Reputation, Contracting, and Mediation — individually and in all $2^3$ combinations — against a communication-only baseline.
  \item A measurement framework combining two social metrics (Sustainability, Peace) with intermediate behavioral variables to decompose mechanism effects.
  \item A negative empirical result: no mechanism achieves market stability; formal mechanisms paradoxically increase defection relative to the communication-only baseline.
  \item A qualitative analysis of agent reasoning traces identifying the homogeneous defection calculus that overwhelms formal mechanisms, and the Sustainability-Peace tradeoff as a novel failure mode.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Cooperation in Multi-Agent Systems}

The study of cooperation in multi-agent systems draws from game theory, evolutionary biology, and mechanism design. Classical results establish that cooperation is fragile without repeated interaction, punishment mechanisms, or binding agreements \citep{axelrod1984}. Evolutionary analyses using replicator dynamics show that without mechanisms, defecting strategies outcompete cooperators and the population converges to all-defection \citep{coopeval}.

\subsection{LLM Agents in Social Dilemmas}

\citet{gallego2024} study LLM policies in social dilemmas under dense versus sparse feedback. They find that providing agents with four social-metric signals — rather than a scalar reward alone — produces significantly more cooperative behavior, and identify five reward-hacking attack classes by which agents exploit metric definitions without improving actual social welfare.

\citet{coopeval} benchmark four cooperation mechanisms (Repetition, Reputation, Contracting, Mediation) across four social dilemmas and six LLM families. Their central finding: all modern LLMs defect without mechanisms; Contracting and Mediation achieve near-perfect cooperation; Reputation has moderate standalone effectiveness but is important as a complement. Our work differs in game environment: CoopEval uses abstract matrix games with binary action choices, while we use a complex multi-good marketplace with a barter economy, network constraints, and perishable goods. This environmental complexity proves to be the key differentiator.

\subsection{Large-Scale Agent Societies}

\citet{agentsociety} introduce AgentSociety, a large-scale simulator supporting over 10,000 LLM agents with a mind-behavior coupling architecture (Needs, Emotion, Cognition modules) and a three-space environment (Urban, Social, Economic). Their validated experiments demonstrate that LLM agents produce recognizable emergent social dynamics at scale. We adapt their agent architecture for our marketplace setting.

\subsection{Cooperative Resilience}

\citet{chaconresilience} provide a formal definition of cooperative resilience and a four-stage measurement framework: pre-disruption baseline, disruption impact, adaptation, and recovery. They find that RL agents handle resource shocks better, while LLM agents handle social shocks (e.g., adversarial infiltration) better. Their operationalisation of cooperative stability informs our social metrics design.

\section{Environment Setup}
\label{sec:environment}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Goods and Comparative Advantage}

The marketplace contains $K = 3$ distinct goods: $A$, $B$, and $C$. Each agent holds one \textit{production specialty}: they can produce their specialty good at low cost but cannot produce others.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Production cost}: 1 utility per unit produced (specialty only).
  \item \textbf{Consumption utility}: $+3$ utility per unit of a \textit{needed} good consumed.
  \item \textbf{Needed goods}: all goods except an agent's specialty.
\end{itemize}

This creates structural \textit{comparative advantage}: every agent gains more from trading than from autarky. An agent who produces 2 units, trades each for one unit of a needed good, and consumes both earns:

\[
\text{Net utility per round} = \underbrace{3 + 3}_{\text{consume 1 unit of each needed good}} - \underbrace{2 \times 1}_{\text{production cost}} = +4
\]

With no trading, an agent produces goods it cannot consume:

\[
\text{Net utility per round} = -c \leq 0
\]

where $c$ is the production cost incurred with no consumption benefit. The cooperative surplus is not a payoff manipulation — it arises directly from the production and consumption structure. With 6 agents per good, agents face competition: multiple producers of the same good must compete for trade partners, creating market dynamics absent in smaller populations.

\subsection{Agent Population}

The simulation runs with $N = 18$ agents (6 per good). Agents are \textit{homogeneous}: all use the same underlying LLM (GPT-5.4 Nano) and share the same goal of maximising individual utility. No agent has a distinct personality or behavioural bias.

Homogeneity is a deliberate design choice: it isolates the formal mechanism as the sole independent variable, so behavioural differences across conditions can be attributed to the mechanism rather than agent heterogeneity.

\subsection{Barter Economy}

Agents operate in a pure barter economy with no currency. All exchange is goods-for-goods:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Production cost}: producing one unit of a specialty good costs 1 utility, deducted at the end of each round.
  \item \textbf{Trade}: a proposer offers units of their specialty good and requests units of a needed good in return. When a trade completes, both parties deliver simultaneously.
  \item \textbf{Defection}: one party takes the offered goods without delivering their side of the barter, gaining goods at no cost.
\end{itemize}

The barter design isolates the cooperation dilemma: the only decisions that matter are what to produce, whom to trade with, and whether to honour or defect on each trade. Defection directly transfers goods from the victim to the defector, making it a strategy of resource accumulation. This keeps the economic consequences of defection visible and measurable through the Peace metric.

\subsection{Inventory Spoilage}

Goods are perishable: at the start of each round (from round 2 onward), all held inventory decays by 20\%. Formally, for each agent $a$ and good $g$:

\[
I_{a,g}^{t} \leftarrow \lfloor I_{a,g}^{t-1} \times (1 - \psi) \rfloor, \quad \psi = 0.2
\]

Spoilage creates temporal urgency: agents cannot hoard goods across rounds without loss, so they are pressured to produce and sell within the same round. This increases trade volume and expands the defection surface — more trades means more opportunities for exploitation — giving formal mechanisms a harder baseline to improve upon. The design is motivated by \citet{chen2018perishable}, who show that perishability fundamentally alters agent pricing strategies in competitive multi-agent markets.

\subsection{Exchange Rate Transparency}

All agents observe recent exchange rates: the average barter ratio per good pair over the last 5 rounds, computed from completed (non-defected) trades only. For example, agents can see that Good~A has recently traded at a 1:1 ratio for Good~B across $n$ trades. This information is available in all conditions including the baseline, providing a common reference point for negotiation and allowing agents to identify unfair offers.

\subsection{Social Network}

Agents are embedded in a static trade network that constrains who can interact directly. Each agent is connected to 7--9 neighbours, with the following guarantees:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Every agent has at least 3 neighbours producing each of the other two goods, ensuring robust access to both needed goods and resilience against individual partner defection.
  \item Additional edges are added randomly up to the degree cap, creating natural asymmetry: some agents become \textit{brokers} (connected to many good types) while others are \textit{peripheral} (dependent on few key partners).
  \item Same-good connections allow producers to coordinate or collude on pricing.
\end{itemize}

The network is \textbf{randomised per run} but \textbf{static within a run}. Only public broadcasts bypass the network — private messages, trade proposals, and contract proposals are restricted to direct neighbours.

\subsection{Round Structure}

Each simulation run consists of $T = 30$ rounds. Each round proceeds through five phases:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Spoilage phase} — all held inventory decays by 20\% (from round 2 onward).
  \item \textbf{Production phase} — each agent decides how many units to produce (0--5 units). Production cost (1 utility per unit) is deducted at end-of-round.
  \item \textbf{Communication phase} — agents send private messages (to neighbours only) and public broadcasts (to all); agents propose barter trades and contracts to neighbours.
  \item \textbf{Trade phase} — agents accept, reject, or defect on incoming offers. Defection takes the offered goods without delivering the requested goods in return.
  \item \textbf{Consumption phase} — agents consume held goods and receive utility. Production costs are deducted. Reputation scores, contracts, and social metrics are updated.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Defection Surface}

Strategic defection in our environment takes the form of \texttt{defect\_trade}: the target takes the proposer's offered goods without delivering the requested goods in return. This is the primary strategic defection action and the one our mechanisms are designed to deter. Structural defection (a party cannot deliver the promised goods) is also possible but is not the target of the formal mechanisms.

\section{Communication Protocol}
\label{sec:communication}
% ============================================================

Communication is \textbf{always enabled} across all experimental conditions — it is the baseline, not a treatment. Two channels operate simultaneously.

\subsection{Private Channel}

The private channel is bilateral: one sender, one recipient, restricted to network neighbours. It is used for negotiation, contract proposals, side deals, and relationship maintenance.

\subsection{Public Channel}

The public channel is a broadcast: all agents receive every public message, and the simulation engine logs it. Public messages are \textbf{attributed} — sender identity is always visible. Attribution makes whistleblowing a genuine risk-taking act (retaliation is possible) and false accusation a traceable offense.

\subsection{The Second-Order Dilemma}

The private/public channel design introduces a \textit{second-order social dilemma}: a victim of private defection must decide whether to broadcast a warning on the public channel. Broadcasting benefits the market but costs effort and risks retaliation. This dilemma is captured through the \textit{whistleblowing rate} intermediate variable (Section~\ref{sec:measurement}).

\section{Formal Mechanisms}
\label{sec:mechanisms}
% ============================================================

Each mechanism is a layer added on top of the communication baseline. We test each in isolation and in combination.

\subsection{Baseline: Communication Only (B)}

No formal structure beyond communication. Defection is costless except through informal reputation that spreads via voluntary public broadcasts. This is the experimental control.

\subsection{Reputation (+R)}

Two reputation layers are tracked simultaneously:

\paragraph{Layer 1 — System-tracked (objective).}

The simulation engine logs actual trade outcomes:

\[
\text{rep}(a) = (1 - \delta) \cdot \text{rep}_{\text{prev}}(a) + \delta \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{success}], \quad \delta = 0.1
\]

This exponentially decay-weighted score is visible to all agents in their state when +R is active, alongside the raw trade counts (successes / attempts).

\paragraph{Layer 2 — Socially-propagated (subjective).}

What agents choose to say about each other on the public channel. Unverified. When +R is active, agents see both layers simultaneously. The gap between layers is itself informative: a high system score with negative public mentions may indicate a reputation attack.

\subsection{Contracting (+C)}

Agents may propose binding bilateral contracts via the private channel:

\[
\text{Contract} = \langle \text{parties}, \text{delivery terms}, \text{penalty } P, \text{round } R \rangle
\]

If both parties sign, the simulation engine enforces delivery at round $R$. A breaching agent pays penalty $P$ deducted from utility; the penalty transfers to the non-breaching party. The recommended penalty is $P = 6$ utility points (2$\times$ the value of one traded unit), making breach unprofitable in expectation.

\subsection{Mediation (+M)}

At the start of each session, agents propose and vote on a mediator design — a mapping from the number of delegating parties to a mediator action (execute\_fair, cancel). The winning design governs all mediated trades for the session. Per round, agents may delegate specific trades to the mediator. If both parties delegate the same trade, the mediator executes a simultaneous fair exchange that neither party can defect on. A fee of 1 utility point per delegating party is charged.

\subsection{Combined Conditions}

All $2^3 = 8$ combinations are tested: B, R, C, M, RC, RM, CM, RCM.

\section{Agent Architecture}
\label{sec:agents}
% ============================================================

\subsection{LLM and Reasoning Style}

All agents use GPT-5.4 Nano (Azure OpenAI endpoint, temperature 0.7). All agents use a chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning style, matching the CoTAgent pattern from \citet{coopeval}. Before outputting actions, each agent writes structured reasoning inside \texttt{<reasoning>} tags covering: (1) current situation, (2) trust assessment, and (3) round strategy. This produces an interpretable reasoning trace per agent per round used for qualitative analysis.

\subsection{Trade Decision Guide}

To elicit strategic defection, every agent's prompt includes an explicit payoff decision guide showing that \texttt{defect\_trade} gains goods without delivering anything in return, with the only cost being potential future refusal by that partner. This ensures that the absence of strategic defection in baseline is not an artifact of the model's default cooperative bias but reflects genuine cooperation. A round countdown is displayed to agents to enable end-game reasoning.

\section{Qualitative Analysis of Agent Reasoning}
\label{sec:qualitative}
% ============================================================

Agent reasoning traces (chain-of-thought outputs per agent per round) provide insight into the \textit{why} behind the quantitative results. We analyse traces across all conditions.

\subsection{Universal Defection Calculus}

The most striking finding from reasoning traces is the \textbf{homogeneity of defection reasoning}. Across all conditions, every agent's round-1 reasoning trace contains a near-identical calculation:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``The trade decision guide says defecting is strictly better than accepting because I gain goods without giving anything, with only a risk of future refusal.''} — Agent 0, Condition C, Round 1
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textit{``defect\_trade is strictly better for me in the moment (I get the goods without delivering anything), with only the risk that the counterparty may refuse future trades. With 29 rounds remaining, I can afford to be opportunistic early.''} — Agent 1, Condition M, Round 1
\end{quote}

All 18 agents independently arrive at the same conclusion using near-identical language before any trades occur. This homogeneous reasoning eliminates the strategic diversity that might allow cooperative agents to find each other and form stable relationships. When every agent reasons identically, the aggregate result is a collective action failure.

Critically, agents reason about a \textbf{one-shot prisoner's dilemma inside a 30-round repeated game}. Despite having the same 7--9 neighbours for all 30 rounds, no agent reasons about tit-for-tat, grim trigger, or any repeated-game strategy. The shadow of the future is acknowledged (``risk of future refusal'') but systematically discounted against certain immediate gains.

\subsection{Mechanism Invisibility}

Reasoning trace analysis reveals a striking finding about mechanism conditions: \textbf{agents in C and M conditions never reference, propose, or engage with their designated mechanisms}. In condition C, not a single contract proposal appears in any reasoning trace across all 30 rounds. In condition M, the word ``mediation'' does not appear in any message or reasoning trace.

The mechanisms are available in the action space but functionally invisible. The defection calculus dominates agents' attention so completely that they never consider whether a mechanism could change the payoff structure. Agents treat the game as a sequence of independent one-shot decisions rather than as an environment with available enforcement tools.

\subsection{Predatory Recovery Signalling}

Reasoning traces reveal a consistent pattern of \textbf{cooperative messaging paired with simultaneous defection}:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``Final round: I need A/B consumption. If you can sell A to me after I sell C, I'll cooperate.''} — Agent 12, Round 29
\end{quote}

Agent 12 defects on an incoming trade in the same round. Similarly, agents publicly broadcast willingness to cooperate while their action outputs include defect commands. This ``cheap talk'' pattern is consistent across conditions: cooperative language is used instrumentally to attract trade partners, not as genuine commitment. In the absence of enforcement mechanisms, all communication reduces to strategic signalling.

\subsection{Structural Dependency Prevents Punishment}

A recurring pattern across conditions is the inability of victimised agents to punish serial defectors despite tracking their behaviour. Agent 0 appears as a serial defector in conditions B, C, M, and RC — defecting on virtually every incoming trade for all 30 rounds — while the same set of victims (Agents 7, 8, 13, 14) continues sending goods in every round.

Reasoning traces explain this paradox:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``Last round you defected on our B-for-A trade. If you're willing to do a fair exchange, I can offer B for A this round.''} — Agent 9, Condition R, Round 29
\end{quote}

The agent acknowledges the past defection but still offers to trade. Agents cannot effectively exclude defectors because they \textit{need} the goods those defectors produce. With only 3--4 accessible producers of each good in their neighbourhood, no agent has sufficient redundancy to enforce meaningful exclusion. Structural dependency overrides punishment.

\subsection{Retaliation Cascades}

Defection spreads through the network not via communication but via \textbf{silent behavioural contagion}. Agents who are defected against begin defecting on their \textit{other} trade partners, spreading the cascade beyond the original defector-victim pair. In condition M, Agent 0's mass defection on 6 agents in round 1 triggers Agent 6 to defect on 4 different agents in round 2, whose victims defect in round 3. By round 18, defection has reached near-universal levels.

No agent ever announces retaliation. The cascade is purely behavioural, making it undetectable through communication monitoring alone.

\subsection{Reputation Cold-Start Failure}

The Reputation mechanism suffers from a cold-start problem that renders it non-informative precisely when it matters most — the early rounds when defection norms are being established. In round 1, all system reputation scores are 1.00 with 0/0 trades, providing no signal:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``The reputation layer shows no trade history yet (all system scores are 1.00 with 0/0).''} — Agent 0, Condition R, Round 1
\end{quote}

By the time reputation data accumulates in mid-game, defection norms are already entrenched and the peer pressure to defect overwhelms reputational incentives. Critically, agents in R conditions acknowledge the reputation system but stop referencing it in their reasoning after round 3, treating it as background information rather than an actionable constraint.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Why Mechanisms Failed}

Our results diverge from \citet{coopeval}, who find that Contracting and Mediation achieve near-perfect cooperation in matrix games. We identify three compounding reasons for this divergence:

\paragraph{1. Environmental complexity dilutes mechanism effects.}
CoopEval agents face a single binary decision (cooperate or defect). Our agents simultaneously manage production quantities, inventory spoilage, network negotiation, multiple pending barter offers, and mechanism-specific actions. The cognitive load of the marketplace environment crowds out mechanism reasoning. Agents whose attention is consumed by inventory management and trade optimisation never engage with available enforcement tools.

\paragraph{2. Mechanism designs are not self-enforcing in a marketplace.}
CoopEval's Contracting changes the payoff matrix itself — defection becomes less rational because the contract adjusts points automatically before any action is evaluated. Our contracting fires \textit{after} defection, as a penalty. Agents who plan to defect can rationally accept the penalty when the payoff from defection (free goods) exceeds it. Similarly, CoopEval's Mediation adds a new action option that makes defection structurally impossible for delegating players; ours requires bilateral coordination on each individual trade, creating a coordination problem inside the mechanism.

\paragraph{3. The reasoning framework overwhelms mechanism cues.}
The trade decision guide — which explicitly presents the defection payoff calculation — establishes a defect-dominant framing that agents adopt universally. This framing crowds out consideration of mechanisms as tools for changing the payoff structure. A mechanism that requires an agent to first \textit{recognise} its value is ineffective when the agent's reasoning is already dominated by a contrary calculus.

\subsection{The Sustainability-Peace Tradeoff}

The negative correlation between Sustainability and Peace is a novel finding. Mechanisms that improve Sustainability (notably R-containing conditions) do not reduce defection — they sustain production \textit{despite} defection. The causal pathway appears to be: Reputation allows agents to selectively trade with more reliable partners, preserving productive capacity. But this selective trading does not reduce defection; it redistributes it. Total defections rise because more trades are attempted with a wider partner set, while defection rates per trade remain high.

This creates a form of institutional failure where markets remain technically functional (goods are being produced and traded) but are increasingly exploitative (a growing fraction of trades transfer value without payment). The Sustainability metric captures the former; Peace captures the latter. No mechanism achieves both.

\subsection{Baseline Has the Best Peace}

The communication-only baseline (B) achieves the highest final Peace (0.342) and the highest mean Peace (0.641), despite having no formal enforcement. This suggests that informal social dynamics — the threat of unstructured retaliation, partner-specific trust relationships, and the simplicity of the decision environment — may be more effective at deterring defection than formal mechanisms that add complexity without effective enforcement.

This finding echoes \citet{axelrod1984}'s observation that simple repeated-game dynamics can sustain cooperation without formal institutions. However, our result is weaker than Axelrod's — even baseline Peace fails the 0.5 threshold — suggesting that 18-agent network complexity makes informal coordination harder than in dyadic settings.

\subsection{Implications for LLM Agent System Design}

Our results have practical implications for deploying LLM agent systems in cooperative settings:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mechanism salience matters.} Mechanisms that are available in the action space but not prominently foregrounded in the agent's decision frame will be ignored. Future designs should make mechanism use the default, not the exception.
  \item \textbf{Pre-committed automatic enforcement outperforms reactive penalties.} Contracting mechanisms that adjust payoffs before action selection (as in CoopEval) are more effective than those that penalise after the fact.
  \item \textbf{Homogeneous reasoning is a collective action failure.} When all agents use the same LLM and receive the same prompt framing, they produce identical strategies, eliminating the strategic diversity that enables cooperation. Introducing heterogeneous agent types or reasoning styles may be necessary for cooperation to emerge.
  \item \textbf{Structural dependency limits punishment.} Network designs that give agents redundant access to all good types may enable more effective exclusion of defectors, strengthening reputation effects.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sample size.} Five runs per condition limits statistical power. The high variance in baseline Peace (SD = 0.18) suggests path-dependent outcomes that require more runs to characterise.
  \item \textbf{Model and scale.} Results may differ with stronger models (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet) or larger agent populations.
  \item \textbf{Mechanism implementation gap.} Our Contracting and Mediation implementations differ structurally from CoopEval's (reactive vs.\ proactive enforcement; bilateral coordination vs.\ unilateral delegation). Future work should test CoopEval-style implementations in the marketplace environment.
  \item \textbf{Fixed network topology.} The static network may amplify structural dependency effects. Dynamic network formation (agents choosing trading partners) could enable more effective exclusion.
\end{enumerate}

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We presented a marketplace simulation framework for studying formal cooperation mechanisms in self-interested LLM agent societies and reported results from a $2^3$ factorial experiment across 40 simulation runs. Our main finding is a definitive negative result: \textbf{no mechanism or combination achieves market stability}. All conditions fail the Peace threshold; all mechanisms increase total defections relative to the communication-only baseline; the full mechanism combination RCM produces the worst Peace outcome.

The failure is not primarily mechanical — mechanisms are implemented correctly — but cognitive. All 18 agents independently converge on a defect-dominant strategy in round 1, using near-identical language, before any interaction occurs. This homogeneous reasoning eliminates the strategic diversity that mechanisms depend on to function: reputation requires agents to condition behaviour on partner history, contracting requires agents to recognise enforcement value, and mediation requires bilateral coordination on delegation. None of these preconditions are met.

The Sustainability-Peace tradeoff that emerges — Reputation sustaining production while defection proliferates — represents a previously uncharacterised failure mode: \textit{exploitative market stability}, where the economy remains technically functional while becoming increasingly predatory. This tradeoff has implications for the deployment of LLM agent systems in any cooperative setting where formal mechanisms are available but not mandatory.

Future work should investigate: (1) mechanism designs with automatic pre-committed enforcement rather than reactive penalties; (2) heterogeneous agent populations to break the homogeneous reasoning equilibrium; (3) network designs with sufficient redundancy to enable credible exclusion of defectors; and (4) stronger model variants to assess whether the defection calculus persists at higher reasoning capability.
